You have probably seen them: a giant perfume bottle rising out of a city street, a sneaker bursting through a billboard, a bus wrapped in a moving product. These are CGI ads, and they have become one of the most effective ways for brands to get noticed in a crowded feed.

Here is what they are, when they make sense, and how to plan one.

## What is a CGI ad?

CGI stands for computer-generated imagery. A CGI ad uses 3D models and animation, either on their own or blended into real footage, to show things that would be impossible or very expensive to film: a product the size of a building, liquid that flows in slow motion around a logo, or an object that assembles itself in mid-air.

There are two common styles:

- **Fully 3D:** everything on screen is built and rendered in software. Common for product showcases and explainer clips.
- **Mixed reality (FOOH):** "fake out of home" ads place a 3D object into real video of a street or landmark, so it looks like a real-world stunt.

## Why CGI ads work

- **They stop the scroll.** Something surprising in the first second makes people pause, and pausing is what platforms reward.
- **They show the product perfectly.** Lighting, angles and materials can be controlled exactly, so the product always looks its best.
- **They are reusable.** Once a 3D model of your product exists, it can be reused for new ads, website visuals and social posts without another shoot.
- **They travel well.** Mixed-reality clips are often shared because people want to ask "is this real?"

## When CGI is a good fit

CGI makes the most sense when:

- your product is physical and visual: packaged goods, fashion, electronics, food and beverage, real estate projects
- you are launching something and need a big first impression
- the idea is impossible or unsafe to film for real
- you want one strong asset that can be cut into many versions for different platforms

It is less useful when the message depends on real people and trust, for example a doctor explaining a treatment. There, a well-shot real video usually works better.

## What does a CGI ad involve?

A typical project moves through these stages:

1. **Concept:** the idea, the hook in the first second, and the message at the end.
2. **Storyboard:** frame-by-frame sketches so everyone agrees before modelling starts.
3. **3D modelling:** building your product and any scene elements accurately.
4. **Animation and lighting:** movement, camera, materials and light.
5. **Compositing:** for mixed reality, blending the 3D work into real footage.
6. **Sound and edit:** music, sound effects and cuts for each platform's length and format.

Changes are cheap during concept and storyboard and expensive after rendering, so most of the thinking should happen early.

## How to brief a CGI ad

Give your agency:

- clear product photos or the original 3D/packaging files if you have them
- the single message the viewer should remember
- where it will run (Instagram Reels, YouTube, outdoor screens) and the lengths needed
- two or three reference ads you like, and why
- your launch date, working backwards to allow time for revisions

## Measure it like any other ad

A CGI ad still has to earn its place. Track hook rate (how many people watch past the first three seconds), view-through rate, shares and saves, and above all what happens after: profile visits, website traffic and sales during the campaign.

## In short

CGI ads are a powerful way to make a product impossible to ignore, especially for launches and visual products. Start with a strong idea and a clear storyboard, and treat the result as a reusable brand asset, not a one-off.
